# Cold Storage & Food Distribution Center — Trinidad and Tobago

> Project plan for an **~800-pallet** cold store on an **~1-acre** site, used to
> **import** frozen and chilled food and sell it to **restaurants and wholesalers**
> in Trinidad and Tobago.
>
> All costs, tariffs and volumes are **planning estimates** to support early
> decisions. Confirm them with local contractors, T&TEC, a customs broker and the
> relevant ministries before you commit any money. Currency conversion used:
> **US$1 ≈ TT$6.8**.

## 2. Market & Product Plan (Months 1–3)

### 2.1 What to stock
Typical lines for the T&T restaurant and wholesale trade:

| Category | Examples | Storage |
|---|---|---|
| Poultry | Chicken leg quarters, wings, breast, whole birds | Frozen −18 °C to −23 °C |
| Beef, pork, lamb | Burger patties, steaks, ribs, ground beef | Frozen |
| Seafood | Shrimp, salmon, fish fillets | Frozen |
| Potato & vegetables | French fries, wedges, mixed vegetables | Frozen |
| Dairy | Cheese, butter, cream | Chilled 0 °C to +4 °C |
| Deli / processed | Ham, bacon, hot dogs, cold cuts | Chilled |
| Bakery & desserts | Frozen dough, buns, ice cream | Frozen (ice cream at −23 °C to −25 °C) |
| Dry support items | Sauces, oils, packaging | Ambient |

### 2.2 Market research to do
- **Customers:** list restaurants, fast-food chains, hotels, caterers, supermarkets and
  smaller wholesalers by area (Port of Spain, East–West Corridor, Chaguanas, San Fernando,
  South, Tobago). Visit 20–30 and record what they buy, how much, how often, from whom,
  at what price, and on what credit terms.
- **Competition:** note the established distributors' product ranges, prices, delivery
  frequency, minimum orders and service gaps. Gaps could be speciality items, reliability,
  next-day delivery or smaller drops.
- **Suppliers:** get quotes from exporters in the USA, Brazil, Canada, the EU and CARICOM.
  Compare landed cost: product + freight + insurance + CARICOM Common External Tariff
  duty + port charges + broker fees + haulage.
- **Target:** before you build, line up letters of intent or trial orders from anchor
  customers covering ~30–40 % of expected volume.

### 2.3 Wholesalers vs. restaurants
| | Wholesalers | Restaurants |
|---|---|---|
| Order size | Full pallets | Mixed cases |
| Margin | Lower (~8–12 %) | Higher (~18–30 %) |
| Handling and delivery cost | Low | High (many small drops) |
| Credit risk | Lower, larger accounts | Higher. Needs strict credit control |

A mix of both keeps volume steady while restaurant sales lift the margin.

### 3.3 Choosing the location (if the land is not yet fixed)
- Within ~30–45 minutes of the port you will use most (Port of Spain or Point Lisas).
- Central to customers. The Chaguanas / Uriah Butler Highway / East–West Corridor
  area reaches most of Trinidad in one delivery shift.
- Outside known **flood zones** (parts of the Caroni basin and low-lying areas flood
  in the rainy season). Check the drainage history.
- **Three-phase power** is available or can be brought in by T&TEC at reasonable cost.
- Good road access for 40 ft container trucks, and a secure neighbourhood.

## 4. Building Design

### 4.1 Rooms and capacity
| Room | Temperature | Size (approx.) | Capacity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Freezer | −20 °C (−25 °C if you stock ice cream) | ~470 m², ~9.5 m clear height | ~560 pallets, selective racking 5 high |
| Chiller | 0 °C to +4 °C | ~250 m² | ~200 pallets, with floor-level pick faces |
| Dry store | Ambient or air-conditioned | ~90 m² | ~40 pallets |
| Chilled dock / pick area | +5 °C to +10 °C | ~350 m² | Receiving, case picking, staging |
| Plant room, electrical, office, amenities | — | ~150 m² (office on mezzanine) | — |

Design points for a hot, humid climate:
- **Keep the cold chain closed.** Put a chilled dock in front of every cold room, use
  dock shelters and insulated dock doors, and fit rapid-roll doors or strip curtains on
  the cold-room doors. In T&T's ~30–33 °C humid air, open doors cause ice build-up,
  frost and high power bills.
- **Insulated panels (PIR):** ~150–175 mm for the freezer, ~100 mm for the chiller
  and dock.
- **Freezer floor:** an insulated slab with underfloor heating or ventilation to
  prevent frost heave. This is mandatory for a freezer; leaving it out cracks the
  floor within a few years.
- **Seismic and wind:** T&T is an active earthquake zone. Have a registered structural
  engineer design the frame, racking anchors and panel fixings to local seismic and
  wind requirements.
- **Roof:** a light-coloured or reflective roof with a ventilated gap above the cold
  rooms reduces heat load.
- **Pallet size:** design the racking for **48″ × 40″ (US) pallets**, the common
  pallet size on US imports.

### 4.2 Refrigeration
- **Load (rough):** ~150–200 kW of cooling in total (freezer ~110–130 kW, chiller
  ~40–50 kW, dock ~25–40 kW). A refrigeration engineer must calculate this properly.
- **Type of system:** at this size, **packaged condensing units or a small compressor
  rack** using a modern refrigerant (e.g., R-448A/R-449A, or propane/R-290 units where
  local technicians are trained for it) is practical and serviceable in T&T. Ammonia
  is not suitable at this scale. CO₂ systems lose efficiency in tropical heat.
- **Redundancy:** use **several units per room** (e.g., 3 × 50 % for the freezer), so
  one failure does not lose temperature.
- **Local service:** choose brands with a **local dealer, spare parts and technicians
  in T&T.** This matters more than a few percent of efficiency.
- Use electronic expansion valves, EC fans and variable-speed compressors, and
  schedule defrosts outside peak loading times.

### 4.3 Power and backup
- **Connected load:** ~250–300 kW → plan a **400–500 kVA** T&TEC supply/transformer.
  Ask T&TEC early, because upgrades can take months.
- **Standby generator:** ~300–400 kVA diesel with an automatic transfer switch and
  ≥ 48 hours of fuel on site. It must run all refrigeration, the dock doors, lights and
  IT. A well-insulated freezer can hold temperature for a few hours with the doors
  closed, but a frozen-food business cannot run without full backup.
- **Energy use:** roughly **350,000–500,000 kWh per year**. T&T electricity is cheap
  by Caribbean standards, so the energy bill is moderate (confirm the current T&TEC
  commercial/industrial tariff and demand charges).
- **Solar PV:** because grid power is cheap, the financial payback is longer than
  elsewhere in the region. Add it later, or for resilience and sustainability rather
  than savings. Leave the roof structure ready for it.
- Surge protection and voltage monitoring on the main board protect the compressors.

### 4.5 Safety and security
- Fire detection suited to cold rooms, extinguishers and hose reels, plus the
  **Trinidad and Tobago Fire Service** certificate.
- "Person-trapped" alarms and inside push-release on every cold-room door.
- Cold-weather PPE (freezer suits, gloves, boots) and timed work/warm-up breaks for
  freezer staff.
- **Security:** perimeter fence, controlled gate, CCTV with off-site recording,
  alarm monitoring, lighting, and no cash kept on site (use electronic payments).
- Comply with the **Occupational Safety and Health Act** (OSHA) and register with the
  OSH Agency.

## 6. Operations

### 6.1 Import cycle
1. **Order** from the supplier. Allow 2–5 weeks lead time depending on origin (US
   Gulf/Florida ≈ 1–2 weeks sailing; Brazil/Europe longer).
2. **Pre-clearance:** your customs broker prepares documents through ASYCUDA. Get
   the import permits and health/veterinary certificates (see §7) **before the
   shipment arrives.**
3. **Port:** the container is inspected (Customs, plus the Chemistry, Food and Drugs
   Division and Veterinary Services where they apply) and released.
4. **Haul** to site and unload straight into the chilled dock. Check the product
   temperature, count against the invoice, record lot and expiry dates, and label.
5. **Put away** to the freezer or chiller, oldest stock (FEFO) to the front.

### 6.2 Sales and delivery cycle
1. Orders come in by app, phone or WhatsApp by a cut-off time (e.g., 4 pm for next-day).
2. **Pick** cases in the chilled dock area, weigh catch-weight items, and print
   invoices and labels.
3. **Load** pre-cooled trucks by route. Record the truck temperature at departure.
4. **Deliver** on fixed route days by area, for example:
   - Mon/Thu: Port of Spain & West
   - Tue/Fri: East–West Corridor & Arima
   - Wed/Sat: Central & South (Chaguanas, San Fernando)
   - Tobago: weekly consolidated shipment via the ferry, or through a Tobago wholesaler
5. Get proof of delivery and collect payment, or follow up on credit.

### 6.3 Inventory and stock levels
- 800 pallets at an average **~70–75 % full** gives room for shipment arrivals and
  seasonal peaks (Carnival, Christmas, Divali and Eid trade).
- Aim for **6–8 stock turns per year** (about 6–8 weeks of stock on hand).
- Review slow movers and expiry dates every week.

## 7. Licences, Permits & Compliance (Trinidad and Tobago)

**Starting the business**
- Company registration (Companies Registry, Ministry of Legal Affairs)
- Board of Inland Revenue: tax registration and **VAT registration (12.5 %)**. Check
  which food items are zero-rated.
- NIS employer registration
- Customs & Excise: importer registration and a **customs broker**
- **Import licences** from the Trade Licensing Unit (Ministry of Trade and Industry)
  for products on the licensed/negative list. Check each HS code.

**Building the facility**
- **Town and Country Planning Division (TCPD):** outline and final planning permission
- **Municipal/Regional Corporation:** building approval
- **Environmental Management Authority (EMA):** confirm whether a Certificate of
  Environmental Clearance (CEC) is needed
- **Trinidad and Tobago Fire Service:** fire plan approval and certificate
- **T&TEC** (power supply) and **WASA** (water, sewer)
- Drainage Division and Highways Division approvals for drainage and road access
- Tobago sites: the Tobago House of Assembly (THA) process

**Food safety and imports**
- **Chemistry, Food and Drugs Division (Ministry of Health):** imported foods must
  meet the Food and Drugs Act and its labelling rules, and are subject to inspection
- **Public Health Department of the Regional Corporation:** food premises
  registration/inspection, and food badges for food handlers
- **Veterinary Services Division (Ministry of Agriculture, Land and Fisheries):**
  import permits and inspection for **meat, poultry and dairy**, with veterinary
  health certificates from the exporting country
- **Fisheries Division / Veterinary Services:** requirements for **seafood**
- **Plant Quarantine Services:** for any fruit and vegetables
- **TTBS** standards where they apply
- Recommended certification once running: **HACCP**, then **ISO 22000 / FSSC 22000**.
  Large hotel and fast-food chain customers often require these.
- **National Ozone Unit / EMA:** refrigerant rules. Use technicians certified for
  refrigerant handling.

> Requirements change. Before you sign contracts, confirm each item with the agency
> or a local attorney / customs broker.

| ERP / IT / scanners | 30k – 80k |
| Design, engineering, permits, project management | 150k – 250k |
| Contingency (~10 %) | 260k – 410k |
| **Total** | **≈ US$2.9 M – 4.5 M (TT$20 – 31 M)** |

**Ways to lower this:**
- Build Phase 1 at ~500 pallets with the walls and plant sized for 800, and add racking
  and refrigeration units later.
- Use a standard steel warehouse shell with **prefabricated modular cold rooms** inside.
- Lease or buy used trucks and forklifts at first.

### 9.2 Working capital (often underestimated)
As an importer you pay for stock **before** you sell it, and restaurants usually expect
**15–30 days of credit**.

| | Year 1 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|
| Stock on hand (~250 → ~580 pallets at ~US$4,000 landed each) | ~US$1.0 M | ~US$2.3 M |
| Money owed by customers (~30 days of sales) | ~US$0.6 M | ~US$1.4 M |
| **Working capital needed** | **~US$1.6 M** | **~US$3.5 M** (partly self-funded from profits) |

**Total funding to plan for in Year 1: ~US$5.0–6.5 M (TT$34–44 M)**, covering
construction, working capital and a cash buffer for pre-opening costs.

### 9.3 Illustrative sales ramp
Assumes 20 pallets per 40 ft container, ~US$4,000 landed cost per pallet, an 18 %
blended gross margin and 6 stock turns. Replace these with your own quotes and prices.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Containers imported | 75 (~1.5/week) | 125 (~2.5/week) | 175 (~3.5/week) |
| Pallets sold | 1,500 | 2,500 | 3,500 |
| Average pallets in storage | ~250 (31 %) | ~420 (52 %) | ~580 (73 %) |
| Sales | ~US$7.3 M | ~US$12.2 M | ~US$17.1 M |
| Gross profit (18 %) | ~US$1.3 M | ~US$2.2 M | ~US$3.1 M |
| Operating costs | ~US$0.9 M | ~US$1.05 M | ~US$1.2 M |
| **EBITDA** | **~US$0.4 M** | **~US$1.15 M** | **~US$1.9 M** |

Operating costs include payroll (~US$0.5–0.75 M), electricity, fuel and truck
maintenance, refrigeration maintenance, insurance, security, IT and admin. EBITDA is
before loan interest, depreciation and corporate tax (30 %).

**The biggest levers on profit are gross margin, how fast you reach 2–3 containers a
week, and bad debts.** In Year 1, consider renting spare space to other importers
(per-pallet storage fees) to cover fixed costs.

### 9.4 Funding options
- Commercial bank term loan for the building (typically 60–70 % of cost) secured on
  the property
- A trade-finance or revolving facility for import purchases (letters of credit,
  supplier credit)
- Equity from owners or partners
- **EXIMBANK** trade and foreign-exchange facilities, and any current government
  incentive or SME programs (check eligibility)
- Supplier credit terms once you have a track record

## 10. Key Risks

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| **Foreign-exchange (USD) availability**, a well-known constraint for T&T importers | Secure bank FX allocations before committing to volume, use EXIMBANK facilities, keep USD-earning revenue where possible, and negotiate supplier credit |
| Power outage | Full-load standby generator, auto transfer switch, fuel stock, alarms, insurance |
| Refrigeration breakdown | Multiple units per room, service contract with 4-hour response, critical spares on site |
| Customer non-payment | Credit applications, limits, COD for new accounts, weekly aged-debt review |
| Shipping delays / port congestion | Safety stock on key lines, more than one supplier and origin, reefer plugs for buffer |
| Flooding | Raised floor level, good site drainage, avoid flood-prone land |
| Earthquake / storms | Engineered structure and racking, insurance |
| Crime / theft | Fencing, CCTV, guards, controlled access, cashless payments, GPS on trucks |
| Price competition from established distributors | Niche or speciality lines, reliability, next-day delivery, smaller minimum orders |
| Food safety incident / recall | HACCP, lot traceability in the ERP, temperature records, product liability insurance |
| Changes to import licences or duties | Track Ministry of Trade notices, and keep a customs broker relationship |

## 13. Next Steps

1. **Survey the 1-acre site:** boundaries, shape, levels, flood history, setbacks,
   road access, and the T&TEC supply nearby.
2. **Build a landed-cost spreadsheet** for your top 20 products using real supplier
   quotes, freight, CET duty, VAT, port and broker fees.
3. **Visit 20–30 target customers** for volumes, prices and terms. Aim for trial orders.
4. **Talk to your bank** about the building loan, trade finance and **USD FX access.**
5. **Engage a local architect and a refrigeration contractor** for a concept layout and
   a firm budget.
6. **Get import requirements confirmed** for your product list (Veterinary Services,
   CFDD, Trade Licensing Unit) through a customs broker.
7. **Start trading early** from rented cold space if possible.
